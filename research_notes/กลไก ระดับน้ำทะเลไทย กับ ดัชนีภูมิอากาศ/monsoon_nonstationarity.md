## Q1. What is WNPMI, what does it measure physically, and how does it relate to ENSO (developing vs decaying years), the IOD and the Indian monsoon?

### Takeaway
WNPMI (Wang & Fan 1999) is a horizontal-shear index of 850-hPa zonal wind: U850 averaged over 5–15°N, 100–130°E minus U850 averaged over 20–30°N, 110–140°E. A high value means stronger monsoon westerlies over the South China Sea, Indochina and the Philippine Sea, with easterlies farther north. That pattern is cyclonic low-level vorticity, which means a deeper monsoon trough. The WNP summer monsoon (WNPSM) is weak in the summer when an El Niño is **decaying**, through the anomalous Philippine Sea anticyclone. The Indian monsoon, by contrast, weakens while an El Niño is **developing**. Since the late 1970s the WNPSM–ENSO link has stayed steady or grown stronger, while the Indian monsoon–ENSO link has weakened.

### Cited Findings
**Definition and variants**
- WNPMI = U850(5–15°N, 100–130°E) − U850(20–30°N, 110–140°E), proposed in Wang & Fan (1999), "Choice of South Asian summer monsoon indices", *Bull. Amer. Meteor. Soc.* 80, 629–638. The same paper also defines a dynamical Indian Monsoon index (IMI). — [ORNL record of Wang & Fan 1999](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/choice-of-south-asian-summer-monsoon-indices/); [NOAA CPC copy of the paper](https://origin.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/Global_Monsoons/Asian_Monsoons/Figures/Index/Wang-Fan_BAMS_1999.pdf)
- NOAA CPC's monsoon-index page lists these related indices:
  - **Dynamic Indian Monsoon Index** = U850(5–15°N, 40–80°E) − U850(20–30°N, 70–90°E), from Wang & Fan (1999).
  - **East Asia–WNP Monsoon Index** = U850(5–15°N, **90**–130°E) − U850(20–30°N, 110–140°E), attributed to Wang et al. (2008, *J. Climate*). Its southern box starts at 90°E, which is wider than the 100°E of the original WNPMI.
  - **Webster–Yang index** = U850 − U200 over 0–20°N, 40–110°E (Webster & Yang 1992, *QJRMS*). This is a vertical-shear index that covers both the Indian and Southeast Asian monsoon areas.
  - **South Asia monsoon (Goswami et al. 1999) index** = V850 − V200 over 10–30°N, 70–110°E (meridional Hadley-type shear).
  - **Australian monsoon index** = U850 over 2.5–15°S, 110–150°E (Hung & Yanai 2004).
  
  — [NOAA CPC: Definition of Monsoon Indices](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/people/qinzhang/monsoon/index/The_difintion_of_Monsoon_Indices.htm)
- Wang & Fan (1999) wrote the paper because the choice of monsoon indices had "received exceptional attention and developed notable controversy". In other words, different indices can capture different parts of the monsoon. — [Search summary of Wang & Fan 1999 / ORNL record](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/choice-of-south-asian-summer-monsoon-indices/)

**WNP vs Indian monsoon: different ENSO timing and different spectra**
- From 50 years of NCEP–NCAR reanalysis, the ISM and the WNPSM show "remarkably different" interannual variability in their spatial–temporal structure, their relationship to El Niño, and their mid-latitude teleconnections. — [Wang, Wu & Lau 2001, *J. Climate* (ORNL record)](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/interannual-variability-of-the-asian-summer-monsoon-contrasts-bet/); [NASA NTRS PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20020016020/downloads/20020016020.pdf)
- Spectral peaks differ between the two systems. The WNP monsoon peaks at about **50 and 16 months**; the Indian monsoon peaks at about **30 months**. — [Wang, Wu & Lau 2001](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20020016020/downloads/20020016020.pdf)
- ENSO phase dependence (this is the developing vs decaying distinction): "the WNPSM is weak during the **decay** of an El Niño, whereas the ISM tends to abate when an El Niño **develops**." — [Wang, Wu & Lau 2001](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/interannual-variability-of-the-asian-summer-monsoon-contrasts-bet/)
- Non-stationarity is already visible in that 2001 paper. Since the late 1970s, "the WNPSM has become more variable, but its relationship with El Niño remained steady: in contrast, the ISM has become less variable and its linkage with El Niño has dramatically declined." — [Wang, Wu & Lau 2001](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/interannual-variability-of-the-asian-summer-monsoon-contrasts-bet/)
- A suppressed WNPSM is teleconnected to deficient US Great Plains summer rainfall through Rossby wave trains. This shows that WNPSM heating anomalies have a large-scale effect. — [Wang, Wu & Lau 2001](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/interannual-variability-of-the-asian-summer-monsoon-contrasts-bet/)

**Philippine Sea anticyclone (Wang, Wu & Fu 2000)**
- The paper is "Pacific–East Asian teleconnection: How does ENSO affect East Asian climate?" (*J. Climate*, 2000). It reports a lower-tropospheric teleconnection between the central Pacific and East Asia during extreme ENSO phases. — [ORNL record](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/pacific-east-asian-teleconnection-how-does-enso-affect-east-asian/)
- The bridge is "an anomalous lower-tropospheric anticyclone (cyclone) located in the western North Pacific". It goes with warm (cold) events and a weak (strong) East Asian winter monsoon. The WNP wind anomalies "develop rapidly in late fall of the year when a strong warm or cold event matures". — [ORNL record of Wang, Wu & Fu 2000](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/pacific-east-asian-teleconnection-how-does-enso-affect-east-asian/)
- The anticyclone is a Rossby-wave response to suppressed convective heating. Two things suppress that heating: local ocean surface cooling, and subsidence forced remotely by central Pacific warming. A positive thermodynamic (wind–evaporation–SST) feedback between the anticyclone and the SST cooling under mean northeasterly trades sustains it. — [ORNL record of Wang, Wu & Fu 2000](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/pacific-east-asian-teleconnection-how-does-enso-affect-east-asian/)
- A companion "Part II: How the Philippine Sea anomalous anticyclone is established during El Niño development" exists (Wang & Zhang). — [ORNL record](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/pacific-east-asian-teleconnection-part-ii-how-the-philippine-sea-/)

**IOD and the Indian monsoon**
- Ashok, Guan & Yamagata (2001, *GRL* 28) studied 1958–1997. The IOD and ENSO affected Indian summer monsoon rainfall in a **complementary** way: when the ENSO–ISMR correlation was low, the IOD–ISMR correlation was high, and vice versa. The ENSO-induced anomalous circulation over India is either countered or reinforced by an IOD-induced anomalous meridional circulation cell. — [Univ. Hyderabad repository](https://dspace.uohyd.ac.in/handle/1/2614); [JAMSTEC PDF](https://www.jamstec.go.jp/aplinfo/sintexf/grl_ashok.pdf)
- Ummenhofer et al. (2011, *Environ. Res. Lett.*), 1877–2006. When an El Niño co-occurs with a positive IOD, Indian Ocean conditions counter El Niño's drought-inducing subsidence by enhancing moisture convergence, and the result is an average monsoon. — [WHOI PDF](https://www.whoi.edu/cms/css/sb/ummenhofer/publications/Ummenhofer.etal_2011_India.pdf)

**WNPMI already appears in SE Asian sea-level work**
- Devlin et al. (2018), "Seasonality of tides in Southeast Asian waters", *J. Phys. Oceanogr.* 48(5), 1169–1190, used 20 tide gauges covering the Malacca Strait, **Gulf of Thailand**, southern SCS and Java Sea. Diurnal and semidiurnal tidal amplitude and phase vary seasonally and are **coherent with WNPMI**, which the authors read as monsoon forcing. A model combining geostrophic and Ekman currents worked best in the southern Gulf of Thailand and near Singapore. — [Search result summarising Devlin et al. 2018 (bepress/PDXScholar)](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/cengin_fac/438)

### Inferences
- **What a WNPMI anomaly means physically.** Positive WNPMI means anomalous westerlies at 5–15°N together with anomalous easterlies at 20–30°N. That is cyclonic shear, so a stronger and deeper monsoon trough and more convection over the SCS and Philippine Sea. Negative WNPMI is the anticyclonic case, which is the signature of the Philippine Sea anticyclone after El Niño. This follows from the index's construction and from Wang et al. (2000, 2001).
- **The southern WNPMI box (100–130°E, 5–15°N) covers the Gulf of Thailand longitudes (≈100–105°E).** So WNPMI partly samples the local wind over the Gulf and southern Indochina. It does **not** sample the Andaman Sea or Bay of Bengal (west of 100°E). For the Andaman coast, the Indian monsoon index boxes or the equatorial Indian Ocean winds are closer to the forcing (see Q2).
- **Seasonal cycle warning.** WNPMI has a large seasonal cycle because its sign reverses between monsoons. Monthly WNPMI that has not been deseasonalised will be coherent with almost any monsoon-affected sea-level series at 12 months. Devlin et al. (2018) found coherence in the *seasonal modulation* of tides. Interannual coherence needs anomalies.
- **Some of any interannual WNPMI–sea-level coherence may really be ENSO.** WNPMI responds to ENSO in the decaying phase (Wang et al. 2001). Partial coherence or conditioning on Niño3.4 would be needed to separate a monsoon-specific path from an ENSO path.

- I found no published study that directly correlates the **WNPMI index** with **interannual Thai tide-gauge mean sea level**. The only WNPMI–sea-level study found concerns tidal properties (Devlin et al. 2018).

  - Wang, Wu & Fu (2000), *J. Climate* 13, 1517–1536, DOI 10.1175/1520-0442(2000)013<1517:PEATHD>2.0.CO;2.
  - Wang, Wu & Lau (2001), *J. Climate* 14, 4073–4090.

As I recall, they report that the post-El Niño Indian Ocean basin warming sustains the NW Pacific anticyclone into the decaying summer, and that this link strengthened after the 1970s. Highly relevant here, but not fetched.

## Q2. How can low-level monsoon wind anomalies over the Bay of Bengal, Andaman Sea and South China Sea affect coastal sea level in Thailand (wind setup, Ekman, coastal Kelvin waves)?

### Takeaway
The two Thai coasts are controlled by different physics.

- **Andaman coast.** It sits on the North Indian Ocean coastal waveguide. Its sea level is dominated (about 80–90% at intraseasonal scales) by **remote equatorial Indian Ocean zonal winds**. Those winds launch equatorial Kelvin waves, which become coastal Kelvin waves travelling around the Bay of Bengal rim into the Andaman Sea. Local monsoon winds add a smaller share.
- **Gulf of Thailand.** It is a shallow (~40 m) semi-enclosed basin. Sea level there responds almost instantly to **local wind stress curl (Ekman pumping)** and to coastal-trapped waves forced by winds farther north. Interannual circulation correlates with the IOD at the western boundary and with ENSO in the interior.

### Cited Findings
**Andaman Sea / Bay of Bengal waveguide**
- Suresh et al. (2013), *GRL* 40(21), 5740–5744, doi:10.1002/2013GL058312. A linear, continuously stratified ocean model reproduces observed wind-driven **intraseasonal** sea level in the North Indian Ocean coastal waveguide.
  - Remote equatorial forcing explains about **80–90% in the Andaman Sea**. The share falls to about 50% northeast of Sri Lanka and rises to 60–70% on the Indian west coast.
  - During the SW monsoon, local wind forcing grows: up to 60% on the Indian west coast and 40% on the east coast.
  
  — [IRD record of Suresh et al. 2013](https://www.documentation.ird.fr/hor/fdi:010061400)
- Coastal Kelvin waves travel along the eastern Bay of Bengal coast and enter the Andaman Sea. Equatorial winds during the monsoon transition seasons drive Kelvin waves along the Andaman coast. — [Search summary drawing on Suresh et al. 2013 and Acta Oceanologica Sinica 2024 (doi:10.1007/s13131-024-2317-8)](https://castjournals.cast.org.cn/joweb/aos/EN/PDF/10.1007/s13131-024-2317-8). This is a secondary summary; the Andaman-specific wording was not checked in the full text.
- Bay of Bengal sea level near the eastern and northern boundaries is "predominantly caused by equatorial wind variability, which generates coastal Kelvin waves that propagate into the bay along the eastern boundary". — [Han & Webster 2002, *J. Phys. Oceanogr.* (PDF)](https://atoc.colorado.edu/~whan/webpage/Publications/hanwebster-jpo02.pdf). Attribution comes from the search snippet; the full text was not opened.
- Two oceanic waveguides meet in the Indonesian throughflow region. Equatorial Indian Ocean wind energy travels along the Sumatra–Java–Nusa Tenggara waveguide into the Indonesian seas. Western Pacific signals travel in the other direction toward the Indian Ocean. — [Wijffels & Meyers 2004, *J. Phys. Oceanogr.* 34(5), 1232–1253 (UTAS eCite)](https://ecite.utas.edu.au/46019)

**Gulf of Thailand**
- Anutaliya (2023), *Ocean Science* 19, 335–350, doi:10.5194/os-19-335-2023. Key results:
  - The Gulf (mean depth ~40 m) is wind-dominated at both seasonal and interannual scales.
  - Basin-averaged absolute dynamic topography (ADT) correlates at **r = −0.84 with wind stress curl, with no lag**. That is an instantaneous Ekman-pumping adjustment, unlike the wider SCS.
  - High-variance ADT on the **western boundary** (the Thai peninsula's east coast) correlates with wind stress curl about 280 km to the north, which is consistent with coastal-trapped Kelvin waves.
  - The **IOD** dominates interannual alongshore currents at the western boundary (45% of meridional-flow variance). **ENSO** correlates with interior circulation (34% of zonal-flow variance at 10–11°N, at a 75-day lag).
  - Data: HF radar 2014–2019 and altimetry 1993–2020.
  
  — [Anutaliya 2023, Ocean Science](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/)
- Interannual tide-gauge variability in the Gulf (for example ENSO) and decadal effects help explain why altimetry and tide-gauge trends differ there. — [Search summary of "Sea level change in the Gulf of Thailand from GPS-corrected tide gauge data and multi-satellite altimetry" (Trisirisatayawong et al.)](https://tccnclimate.com/เอกสารงานวิจัย/เอกสารตีพิมพ์/item/147-sea-level-change-in-the-gulf-of-thailand-from-gps-corrected-tide-gauge-data-and-multi-satellite-altimetry)

### Inferences
These are physical reasoning, not drawn from a Thai-specific paper.
- **Pathway A: remote equatorial winds → Andaman sea level.** This is likely the dominant interannual path. Positive IOD and El Niño years tend to bring **anomalous easterlies** over the eastern equatorial Indian Ocean. Those generate **upwelling** (negative-SLA) equatorial Kelvin waves that reflect into coastal Kelvin waves along Sumatra, Myanmar and the Andaman coast, so Thai Andaman sea level should drop during El Niño and positive-IOD years. That links Andaman sea level to SOI and Niño3.4 *through* Indian Ocean winds, not through the WNP monsoon. Suresh et al. (2013) quantified only intraseasonal shares; the interannual share is not established here.
- **Pathway B: local SW monsoon wind setup and Ekman transport on the Andaman coast.** Phuket and Ranong face west to southwest. During the SW monsoon, onshore winds pile water against the coast. The southerly alongshore component, with the coast to its right, drives Ekman transport onshore in the Northern Hemisphere. Both effects raise coastal sea level. The NE monsoon does the reverse. Interannual changes in SW monsoon strength (the IMI or Bay of Bengal U850) should therefore change seasonal-mean Andaman sea level. This contribution is likely smaller than Pathway A (compare the 40% local share for India's east coast in Suresh et al. 2013).
- **Pathway C: SCS and Indochina winds (the WNPMI region) → Gulf of Thailand.** Local wind stress curl in the Gulf matters (r = −0.84, Anutaliya 2023). NE-monsoon winds push water toward the southwestern Gulf, the east coast of the Thai peninsula. WNPMI anomalies, especially the post-El Niño Philippine Sea anticyclone with anomalous easterlies or northeasterlies over the SCS, should mainly affect **Gulf** stations and much less the Andaman stations.
- **The Pacific ENSO signal through the Indonesian throughflow** mainly reaches NW Australia (see Q3/Q4, Nidheesh et al.). It is an unlikely direct path to the Andaman Sea, which connects to the equatorial Indian Ocean waveguide rather than the Indonesian throughflow outflow.
- **Inverse barometer effect.** Sea-level-pressure anomalies tied to the monsoon and ENSO (the SOI is itself a pressure difference) affect sea level at about 1 cm per hPa. This is small but correlated with SOI. Standard physics; not sourced this session.

### Gaps
- I did not find a peer-reviewed study that quantifies **interannual** Andaman (Thai) tide-gauge sea level against equatorial IO winds, the IOD or ENSO. The best evidence is intraseasonal (Suresh et al. 2013) or about currents in the Gulf (Anutaliya 2023).
- **Background, unverified. Check before citing:** Saramul & Ezer (2014), "Spatial variations of sea level along the coast of Thailand: impacts of extreme land subsidence, earthquakes and the seasonal monsoon", *Global and Planetary Change* 122, 70–81. As I recall it covers monsoon-driven seasonal sea level and earthquake-related vertical land motion at Thai gauges. My search did not return it. If confirmed, it is the most directly relevant Thai paper. It also matters for Q3, because post-2004 Sumatra earthquake land motion at Andaman gauges could reduce coherence in long records.

## Q3. Is there evidence that ENSO–IOD–monsoon teleconnections, including ENSO's influence on Indian Ocean sea level, change across decades?

### Takeaway
Yes, the evidence is strong. Three changes stand out:

- **Indian monsoon.** The inverse ENSO–Indian monsoon relationship weakened sharply after the late 1970s (Kumar et al. 1999). This has been attributed to shifts in the Walker circulation, Eurasian warming and offsetting by the IOD.
- **WNP/East Asian/Indonesian monsoons.** Their coupling with ENSO **strengthened** after the late 1970s (Wang et al. 2008).
- **IOD.** Positive IOD events became more frequent and stronger in the late 20th century (Abram et al. 2008; Cai et al. 2009).

Pacific decadal variability (PDO/IPO) modulates how ENSO projects onto the monsoon. Together these give a plausible reason why coherence of Thai Andaman sea level with SOI/Niño3.4 could be stronger in 1985–2019 than across 1951–2023. However, no study tests this for Thai sea level directly.

### Cited Findings
**Weakening ENSO–Indian monsoon link**
- Kumar, Rajagopalan & Cane (1999), "On the weakening relationship between the Indian monsoon and ENSO", *Science* 284(5423), 2156–2159. A 140-year record shows the inverse ENSO–ISMR relationship "has broken down in recent decades". They proposed two mechanisms:
  1. A southeastward shift of ENSO-related Walker circulation anomalies, which reduces subsidence over India.
  2. Warmer Eurasian winter and spring surface temperatures, which strengthen the land–ocean thermal gradient.
  
  — [IAS repository record](https://repository.ias.ac.in/90480); [CU Boulder VIVO](https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_17402)
- Wang, Wu & Lau (2001): after the late 1970s the ISM–El Niño linkage "dramatically declined", while the WNPSM–El Niño relationship "remained steady". — [ORNL record](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/interannual-variability-of-the-asian-summer-monsoon-contrasts-bet/)

**Strengthening ENSO–WNP/EA/Indonesian monsoon link**
- Wang, Yang & Zhou (2008), "Interdecadal changes in the major modes of Asian–Australian monsoon variability: strengthening relationship with ENSO since the late 1970s", *J. Climate*. They applied season-reliant EOF analysis to 1956–2004.
  - "Since the late 1970s the overall coupling between the A–AM system and ENSO has become strengthened." The ENSO relationships with the **western North Pacific, East Asian and Indonesian monsoons** grew stronger in ENSO's developing, mature and decaying phases.
  - Before the late 1970s (1956–79), mode 1 had a strong biennial tendency and mode 2 did not lead ENSO.
  
  — [ORNL record](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/interdecadal-changes-in-the-major-modes-of-asian-australian-monso/)

**The IOD as modulator, and more IOD activity**
- Ashok et al. (2001): ENSO–ISMR and IOD–ISMR correlations alternated over 1958–1997, so the IOD modulates the ENSO–monsoon relationship. — [Univ. Hyderabad repository](https://dspace.uohyd.ac.in/handle/1/2614)
- Ummenhofer et al. (2011): decadal changes in how often El Niño and positive IOD occur alone or together are consistent with two epochs. The El Niño–Indian monsoon relationship was strong in the early 20th century and has weakened recently. — [WHOI PDF](https://www.whoi.edu/cms/css/sb/ummenhofer/publications/Ummenhofer.etal_2011_India.pdf); [IOP record](https://ioppublishing.org/sdg_article/multi-decadal-modulation-of-the-el-nia%c2%b1oaeindian-monsoon-relationship-by-indian-ocean-variability/)
- Abram et al. (2008), "Recent intensification of tropical climate variability in the Indian Ocean", *Nature Geoscience*.
  - A coral Dipole Mode Index back to 1846 suggests positive IOD events grew stronger and more frequent during the 20th century.
  - Mentawai fossil corals suggest four extreme positive IODs in the last ~60 years, against about six in the preceding ~1000 years.
  
  — [Univ. of Arizona experts record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/recent-intensification-of-tropical-climate-variability-in-the-ind/); [Wikipedia summary (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_Dipole)
- Cai, Sullivan & Cowan (2009), "Climate change contributes to more frequent consecutive positive Indian Ocean Dipole events", *GRL*. The 19-model CMIP3 mean IOD index for 1950–1999 trends upward, and the associated circulation trends give about **17% more positive IODs** than with the trends removed. — [USQ repository](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/z09w6/climate-change-contributes-to-more-frequent-consecutive-positive-indian-ocean-dipole-events); [Wikipedia summary (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_Dipole)

**Pacific decadal modulation of the ENSO–monsoon link**
- Krishnamurthy & Krishnamurthy (2014, *Clim. Dyn.*), plus related GMU work. A decadal mode of about 21 years is tied to the **PDO**. When ENSO and the PDO are in phase they reinforce the ENSO–monsoon relationship; out of phase they counteract it. El Niño with positive PDO tends to mean India-wide deficit; La Niña with negative PDO tends to mean surplus. The evidence combines long observations with CCSM4 experiments. — [GMU MARS record](https://mars.gmu.edu/items/045543d7-ddb1-499a-b2f8-831ec99f02eb); [IAS repository (PDO and ISMR)](https://repository.ias.ac.in/109438)

**Decadal sea level in the Indo-Pacific**
- Nidheesh et al. (2017), "Robustness of observation-based decadal sea level variability in the Indo-Pacific Ocean", *GRL*.
  - Decadal sea level is robust in the Pacific, where more than 50% of its variance comes from decadal modulation of the canonical and Modoki ENSO flavours.
  - Indian Ocean decadal sea level is **weaker**.
  - Decadal signals travel from the western Pacific to the **NW Australian coast through the Indonesian throughflow**.
  - The southern tropical Indian Ocean signal links to ENSO decadal modulation in reconstructions but not in reanalyses or in-situ data. Pacific-independent Indian Ocean decadal variability is not robust.
  
  — [Archimer (Ifremer) record](https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00395/50593)
- In CMIP models, a decadal IOD is more independent of ENSO (r ≈ 0.4) than the interannual IOD (r ≈ 0.6). Decadal IOD–ENSO co-variability produces sea-level signals along west Australia via the Indonesian throughflow. — [IAS repository: "Natural decadal sea-level variability in the Indian Ocean: lessons from CMIP models"](https://repository.ias.ac.in/123903/)

### Inferences
Possible explanations for stronger Andaman–SOI/Niño3.4 coherence in 1985–2019 than over 1951–2023. These are speculative and need testing.
1. **Regime selection.** The 1985–2019 window lies wholly after the late-1970s shift, when ENSO coupling with the WNP, Indonesian and A–AM monsoons was stronger (Wang et al. 2008). The 1951–2023 window averages across both regimes, which dilutes coherence.
2. **ENSO–IOD co-occurrence and stronger IOD activity in the late 20th century** (Abram 2008; Cai 2009). Andaman sea level is fed by equatorial Indian Ocean winds (Q2). If ENSO events after 1985 more often came with IOD-type equatorial easterly anomalies, ENSO's imprint on Andaman sea level would be amplified in that window. The window includes large El Niño and positive-IOD years such as 1997/98 and 2015/16. That event list is from background knowledge, not sourced here.
3. **A few large events dominate wavelet coherence.** Extreme El Niños (1997/98, 2015/16) inside the window can drive high coherence in the 2–7 yr band. In a longer record, epochs of weak coupling (for example the 1950s–70s) lower the average.
4. **Data quality and non-climatic signals in long tide-gauge records.** Early-record gaps, datum changes and vertical land motion can add incoherent noise to 1951–2023. Two examples:
   - Subsidence and post-seismic motion after the 2004 Sumatra–Andaman earthquake, if the Saramul & Ezer (2014) finding is confirmed.
   - Pre-satellite-era reanalysis winds being less reliable before 1979, which matters for any wind-based index such as WNPMI.
5. **PDO/IPO phase.** Under Krishnamurthy & Krishnamurthy (2014), ENSO teleconnections strengthen when ENSO and the PDO are in phase. 1985–2019 spans a positive-PDO phase (to ~1998) and a negative one (~1999–2013). The net effect on this window is unclear, so this is a modulator to test, not a ready explanation.
6. **Inconsistency to keep in mind.** The *Indian* monsoon–ENSO link **weakened** after the late 1970s, while ENSO's links with the WNP and Indonesian monsoons strengthened. The Andaman coast sits between these systems. Which side dominates for Andaman sea level is an open empirical question.

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly documents a post-1980s or post-1990s **strengthening of ENSO influence on eastern Indian Ocean or Andaman sea level**. The non-stationarity evidence covers rainfall, monsoon circulation and SST modes, not Thai sea level.
- **Background, unverified. Check before citing:**
  - Kumar et al. (2006, *Science* 314, 115–119), "Unraveling the mystery of Indian monsoon failure during El Niño". As I recall, central-Pacific-warming El Niños are more often tied to Indian drought.
  - Xie et al. (2010, *J. Climate*), a 1970s decadal shift in El Niño influence on the Indo–western Pacific.

## Q4. Why would PDO, TPI, AMO and NAO show weak interannual coherence with Thai sea level?

### Takeaway
These are mostly **decadal-to-multidecadal** modes. The PDO/IPO/TPI vary over roughly 10–30 years and the AMO over roughly 60–80 years. The NAO and AMO are Atlantic modes that have no direct ocean waveguide or strong atmospheric bridge to the Andaman Sea or Gulf of Thailand. In the literature, the PDO/IPO acts as a **modulator** of how strongly ENSO projects onto the monsoon and onto Indo-Pacific sea level. It is not a direct interannual driver. Weak interannual coherence is therefore the expected result, not a failure of the analysis.

### Cited Findings
- The PDO-linked decadal mode (about a 21-year period) works by strengthening or weakening the ENSO–monsoon relationship depending on phase alignment, not as an independent interannual forcing. — [Krishnamurthy & Krishnamurthy (GMU record)](https://mars.gmu.edu/items/045543d7-ddb1-499a-b2f8-831ec99f02eb)
- Pacific decadal sea level is largely decadal modulation of ENSO (more than 50% of variance). In the Indian Ocean its expression is weak, and it reaches mainly the NW Australian coast via the Indonesian throughflow, not the northern Indian Ocean. — [Nidheesh et al. 2017 (Archimer)](https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00395/50593)
- Pacific-independent decadal sea-level variability in the Indian Ocean "is not robust" across data sets. — [Nidheesh et al. 2017 (Archimer)](https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00395/50593)

### Inferences
1. **Time-scale mismatch and statistics.** A 35-year window such as 1985–2019 holds only about one to three PDO/IPO cycles and less than one AMO cycle. Most of these indices' power lies at periods near or outside the wavelet cone of influence. Coherence in the 2–7 yr band is limited by how much interannual power the indices carry, and that is small for the AMO and the low-pass-defined TPI. Any significant coherence at 10–30 yr would rest on very few degrees of freedom.
2. **The PDO's interannual content is partly ENSO.** Any interannual PDO–sea-level coherence probably duplicates the ENSO signal rather than adding independent information. A partial-coherence test conditioned on Niño3.4 would settle this.
3. **The NAO and AMO are remote.** Their effect on the Asian monsoon runs through Eurasian wave trains or Atlantic–Indian SST bridges, which act mainly on rainfall and on decadal scales. No oceanic path connects them to Andaman or Gulf of Thailand sea level. Weak coherence is expected physically. The NAO is also dominated by intraseasonal "noise", so its monthly index has a nearly white spectrum, which further limits coherence.
4. **The TPI (IPO tripole index)** is built to capture the IPO. When computed from low-pass-filtered SST, its interannual variance is deliberately suppressed. Raw or monthly TPI keeps ENSO content, so the same point as for the PDO applies.
5. **Pacific decadal sea-level signals leak into the Indian Ocean through the Indonesian throughflow toward NW Australia** (Nidheesh et al. 2017), away from the Andaman Sea. That is a geographic reason why Pacific decadal modes should matter less for Thai Andaman gauges than for Western Australian ones.
