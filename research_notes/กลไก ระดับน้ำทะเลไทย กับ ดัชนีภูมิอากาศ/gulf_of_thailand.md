# Gulf of Thailand sea level: monsoon dominance and weak coupling to climate indices

Context from our data (Ko Lak, PSMSL, ~11.8°N 99.8°E): seasonal cycle ≈ 81% of variance, annual amplitude ≈ 213 mm; interannual residual (after removing seasonal cycle and trend) shows little or no coherence with SOI, Niño3.4, DMI, PDO or monsoon indices. The notes below collect published evidence for interpreting that result.

## Q1. What drives the large annual cycle in Gulf of Thailand sea level? Typical amplitudes and phases

### Takeaway
The Gulf of Thailand (GoT) has one of the largest seasonal sea-level cycles in the South China Sea region: ~20–27 cm annual amplitude, high in the NE monsoon (peak around early/mid January) and low in the SW monsoon (minimum around July). It is mainly a barotropic **mass** signal set up by monsoon winds (wind stress/Ekman transport and wind-stress-curl Ekman pumping on a shallow shelf), with an atmospheric-pressure contribution; steric effects are secondary. Our Ko Lak amplitude (~213 mm) and ~81% seasonal variance are fully in line with the literature.

### Cited Findings
- GoT is a shallow, semi-enclosed basin at 8–14°N, 99–105°E with average depth ~40 m, "largely influenced by winds on both seasonal and interannual timescales"; SW monsoon ≈ May–Aug (wet), NE monsoon ≈ Nov–Feb (dry) — [Anutaliya 2023, Ocean Sci.](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- GRACE ocean-bottom-pressure shows a strong seasonal cycle in the GoT with amplitude ≈ 20 cm peaking in early January; "mass related variations dominate sea level in this region"; agrees with altimetry and a nearby tide gauge; a baroclinic ocean model underestimates signal power by ~30% — [Wouters & Chambers 2010, Global Planet. Change (KNMI abstract page)](https://www.knmi.nl/research/publications/analysis-of-seasonal-ocean-bottom-pressure-variability-in-the-gulf-of-thailand-from-grace)
- Across SCS coasts the mean annual amplitude ranges 0–24 cm (maximum between July and January); max semiannual amplitude 7 cm (peak Mar–Jun). Atmospheric pressure explains a significant part of the annual signal in the northern SCS, GoT and NW Philippines; **wind forcing is dominant on the SCS shelf areas and the GoT**, where a simple barotropic model forced by local wind gives amplitudes up to 27 cm. Steric is the major driver only in deeper basins (Philippine Sea, Malacca Strait; ≤15 cm). Annual and semiannual amplitudes themselves vary year-to-year by up to 63% and 45% (of max 15 and 11 cm); regression on forcing factors explains 66% of annual-cycle variability — [Amiruddin et al. 2015, JGR Oceans (NORA record)](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/511518/)
- Along the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia / GoT the seasonal cycle is ±25 cm, single peak during NE monsoon and trough during SW monsoon; "the NE wind causes sea level elevation in the Gulf of Thailand" (winter); seasonal SLAs are "mainly monsoon-driven, in the order of 10–25 cm" — [Luu et al. 2015 (discussion paper text)](https://os.copernicus.org/preprints/11/1519/2014/osd-11-1519-2014.pdf)
- Altimetry 1993–2012: GoT SSH seasonal amplitude is larger than in the open SCS; maximum phase in mid-January, minimum in July — [Naeije, Niyamnil & Trisirisatayawong 2018 (Thai journal)](https://ph01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/rtna/article/view/240088)
- Basin-averaged GoT ADT is high in the NE monsoon and low in the SW monsoon and is anti-correlated with basin-averaged wind stress curl (r = −0.84) with **no lag** — "instantaneous adjustment" via local Ekman pumping, unlike the rest of the SCS where ADT responds to curl with a ~40-day decay timescale (Zhou et al. 2012) — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- On the GoT **western boundary** (where Ko Lak lies), ADT at 9.6°N 99.9°E is best correlated with wind-stress curl ~280 km to the north (12.1°N 100.1°E), which explains 80% of its variance; the author suggests equatorward-propagating coastal-trapped Kelvin waves along the western boundary — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- GoT circulation reverses seasonally with the monsoon (leading CEOF = 28% of circulation variance); during monsoon transitions a strong meridional western-boundary current connects to the SE entrance, implying high GoT–SCS exchange — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- Satellite ADT vs 7 GoT tide gauges (Fort Phrachula, **Ko Lak**, Ko Mattaphon, Hua Hin, Ko Prap, Laem Sing, Ko Sichang), 2014–2019: r = 0.69–0.94; weakest at Fort Phrachula due to groundwater-driven land subsidence and its ~20 km inland location — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- Seasonal circulation in the GoT has been documented by Yanagi et al. 2001, Buranapratheprat et al. 2002/2008, Aschariyaphotha et al. 2008, Saramul & Ezer 2014 (Ocean Dynamics, numerical simulations of the upper GoT), Saramul 2017, Sojisuporn et al. 2010 (TOPEX/ERS-2 dynamic height 1995–2001) — as reviewed in [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- A search-engine summary attributed to a 50-year (1971–2020) PSMSL Gulf of Thailand tide-gauge study (IEEE Access 2022, "Spatial Changes of Ocean Circulation Along the Coast of Gulf of Thailand Using Tide Gauge Measurements", KMITL) the statement that the seasonal cycle accounts for "up to 92% of monthly sea level variability" on the continental shelf — [KMITL repository record](https://dspace.kmitl.ac.th/handle/123456789/16296); **not verified** (full text inaccessible, 403/404), attribution of the 92% number to this exact paper is uncertain.

### Inferences
- Mechanism summary (well established): NE monsoon (Nov–Feb) northeasterlies over the SCS drive onshore/westward Ekman transport toward the Sunda Shelf/GoT and positive set-up inside the semi-enclosed shallow gulf → high water in Dec–Jan; SW monsoon reverses this → set-down and minimum in Jun–Aug. Because the GoT is shallow (~40 m mean), wind stress acts on a thin water column, so the set-up is large and barotropic (hence GRACE sees it as mass).
- Steric and river discharge are second-order for the GoT annual cycle: Wouters & Chambers (mass dominates) and Amiruddin et al. (wind dominates on shelf; steric dominant only in deep basins) both support this. River discharge (Chao Phraya, Mae Klong, Tha Chin, Bang Pakong) is concentrated in the upper Gulf and peaks in Sep–Nov; it plausibly matters near Fort Phrachula but not at Ko Lak (inference, not quantified in sources found).
- Ko Lak's ~213 mm annual amplitude is at the high end of the 20–27 cm published range; ~81% seasonal variance is consistent with the "up to 92%" shelf figure. The implication is that the non-seasonal residual is only ~19% of total variance, and some of that is intraseasonal wind noise and annual-cycle modulation, not interannual climate signal.

### Gaps
- Could not access Saramul & Ezer (2014, Ocean Dynamics) or Sojisuporn et al. (2010) full texts to extract numeric amplitudes/phases at specific stations.
- No source found that quantifies river-discharge or steric contributions to sea level at Ko Lak specifically.

## Q2. Published evidence of ENSO-related interannual sea-level anomalies in the SCS and in the Gulf of Thailand (sign, magnitude, lag)

### Takeaway
For the SCS as a whole the evidence is strong: basin-mean interannual sea level **falls during El Niño and rises during La Niña**, lagging ENSO by ~2–6 months, with maximum correlations ~0.75–0.89 (altimetry, basin-mean or leading-mode). Mechanisms are ENSO-driven wind anomalies over the SCS interior and Pacific Rossby/Kelvin waves entering through the Luzon and Mindoro straits, which act mostly on the **eastern** SCS. For the Gulf of Thailand specifically, evidence is thin, short-record, and spatially mixed (even sign differences between sub-regions); magnitudes of a few cm at most.

### Cited Findings
- SCS (altimetry 1993–2004): sea level decreases during El Niño, increases during La Niña; ENSO acts through atmospheric circulation changes and Kuroshio intrusion through the Luzon Strait, producing a lagged response — Rong et al. 2007, as summarized in [Yang & Fok 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1467164/full)
- Reported max cross-correlations: Rong et al. 2007 = 0.78 with SOI (SOI leads by 4 months); Soumya et al. 2015 = 0.75 with SOI (2-month lead); Peng et al. 2013 = 0.89 with Niño4 (Niño4 leads by 6 months); Cheng et al. 2016: significant correlation with Niño3.4 leading by 6 months **during El Niño decaying summers** — [Yang & Fok 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1467164/full)
- Peng et al. 2013 (reconstruction 1950–2009 + altimetry 1993–2009 + 14 tide gauges): interannual SCS mean sea level is highly correlated with Niño4, "suggesting that the interannual sea level variations over the region is driven by ENSO events" — [search summary of Peng et al. 2013, Marine Geodesy 36:164–182](https://hal-univ-tlse3.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00991287)
- Cheng et al. 2016: interannual SCS sea level "shows a strong seasonality"; ENSO surface-wind anomalies explain the SLA pattern in the **interior** SCS, while Rossby waves radiated from the **eastern boundary** dominate the eastern SCS; decadal SCS-mean sea level correlates with PDO at r = −0.96 (1993–2012) and PDO-related decadal variability explains most of the 1993–2012 SCS trend — [Cheng et al. 2016 abstract (OUC page)](https://pol.ouc.edu.cn/2020/1028/c18673a304945/page.htm)
- Mechanism for the eastern SCS: during El Niño, upwelling off-equatorial Rossby waves hit the Philippines, enter the SCS through the Mindoro Strait as upwelling coastal Kelvin waves propagating north along the Philippine west coast → negative SLA in the SE SCS and larger Mindoro/Luzon transports; signal at ~4-month lead, decays by ~8 months; Luzon and Mindoro transport anomalies correlate ~0.7 with Niño3.4 (2-month lag) but **"the Karimata Strait transport is not significantly correlated with the Niño 3.4 index"** — [Liu, Feng & Wang 2011, J. Oceanogr.](https://per.marine.csiro.au/staff/Ming.Feng/Publications/2011LiuJO.pdf)
- Max ENSO–SLA cross-correlation is greater along the SCS (south)eastern coastal boundaries than in the central SCS; weak interior correlation is linked to ENSO wind-curl anomalies and local winds that can damp SLA variability; during mature El Niño an anomalous anticyclone over the SCS induces downwelling in the central basin — [Yang & Fok 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1467164/full)
- Ocean mass in the SCS decreases in El Niño and increases in La Niña (controlled via precipitation and Kuroshio/Luzon transport); steric SCS trend 1993–2012 ≈ 4.7 ± 0.3 mm/yr with steric 40–55% — [Xi et al. 2019, J. Coastal Res. 35:489–498](https://bioone.org/journals/journal-of-coastal-research/volume-35/issue-3/JCOASTRES-D-18-00080.1/Long-Term-and-Interannual-Variation-of-the-Steric-Sea-Level/10.2112/JCOASTRES-D-18-00080.1.full) (from search snippet; full text not read)
- Peninsular Malaysia tide gauges (nearest well-studied analogue to the southern GoT): ENSO modulates sea level by ±5 cm, correlation ≈ −0.7 with MEI (falls during El Niño, rises during La Niña); east-coast stations average r = −0.64; during extreme El Niño/La Niña annual sea level changes on average ~3 cm on the east coast and ~4 cm in Malacca Strait. **Note:** these correlations were computed on *annual-mean* sea level, not monthly — [Luu et al. 2015 (discussion text)](https://os.copernicus.org/preprints/11/1519/2014/osd-11-1519-2014.pdf)
- Singapore Strait SLA correlates −0.7 with MEI, ±5 cm in El Niño/La Niña (Tkalich et al. 2013) — cited in [Luu et al. 2015](https://os.copernicus.org/preprints/11/1519/2014/osd-11-1519-2014.pdf)
- Gulf of Thailand, altimetry 1993–2012 (Thai-language study): ENSO events lowered sea level in the GoT and SCS — [Naeije, Niyamnil & Trisirisatayawong 2018](https://ph01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/rtna/article/view/240088)
- Gulf of Thailand, remote sensing 2014–2019 (6 years, 90-day low-passed, seasonal harmonics removed): ENSO correlates with the **interior** GoT circulation; Niño3.4 explains 34% of low-frequency zonal current variance at ~10–11°N, 100.5–103°E, rising to r = −0.71 with a 75-day lag; cyclonic anomaly with low ADT in the GoT interior during El Niño. But in the **upper GoT** ADT is positively correlated with Niño3.4 (anomalously *high* sea level, especially along the eastern boundary, during El Niño), consistent with the wind-curl pattern. Correlation of Niño3.4 with the western-boundary meridional current is small (R = 0.14) and "not clearly apparent during the 2015/2016 El Niño and the 2017/2018 La Niña" — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- Trisirisatayawong et al. 2011: differences between altimetry-based rates and absolute tide-gauge trends can be explained by interannual variations like ENSO and decadal variations — [TCCN record of Trisirisatayawong et al. 2011](https://tccnclimate.com/เอกสารงานวิจัย/เอกสารตีพิมพ์/item/147-sea-level-change-in-the-gulf-of-thailand-from-gps-corrected-tide-gauge-data-and-multi-satellite-altimetry)
- PDO: Soumya et al. 2015 reportedly found the PDO accounts for ~30% of interannual SLA in the SE SCS and the **northern Gulf of Thailand**, and in the SW SCS ENSO contributes ~30% vs IOD ~40% — second-hand via [Yang & Fok 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1467164/full) (primary not verified)
- Regional SE Asian seas: removing the PDO contribution greatly reduces 20-year sea-level-rise rates — search snippet summarising a regional study, [Southampton eprint 415492](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/415492) (paper not identified/verified; treat as weak).

### Inferences
- Well established: SCS basin-mean (deep-basin, altimetric) sea level has a robust negative ENSO relationship (low in El Niño), lag 2–6 months, amplitude of order ±5 cm near the Malaysian/Singapore coasts.
- Not established: a robust, quantified ENSO signal in GoT tide-gauge sea level. The only GoT-specific modern analysis (Anutaliya 2023) uses 6 years, finds ENSO links in circulation in the interior, IOD dominance along the western boundary, and opposite-sign ADT responses in the upper GoT vs interior. A 6-year record contains only ~1–2 ENSO cycles, so these correlations are suggestive rather than definitive.
- Expected sign at Ko Lak, if any ENSO signal exists: ambiguous. SCS-wide studies imply a fall during El Niño; Anutaliya's upper-GoT ADT implies a rise near the upper-gulf eastern side. Ko Lak (western boundary, ~11.8°N) sits between regimes, which is itself a reason for a weak/ambiguous correlation.
- Expected magnitude: a few cm (≈ 2–5 cm), i.e. ~10–25% of the 213 mm annual amplitude, and comparable to year-to-year modulation of the annual cycle itself (Amiruddin: annual amplitude varies by up to 63% of 15 cm).

### Gaps
- No paper found that directly correlates a Gulf of Thailand tide gauge (Ko Lak, Sattahip, Ko Sichang, Songkhla, Fort Phrachula) monthly interannual residual with SOI/Niño3.4/DMI/PDO and reports r, lag and significance.
- Soumya et al. 2015 primary text (which reportedly covers the GoT) and Peng et al. 2013 station-level results were not accessible; Rong et al. 2007 lag/correlation values are second-hand.
- A Mahidol repository item "ENSO may have contributed to sea level changes in the Gulf of Thailand during the Late-Holocene" exists ([Mahidol repository](https://repository.li.mahidol.ac.th/handle/123456789/90567)) but concerns paleo (millennial) sea level, not modern interannual variability.

## Q3. Why might Gulf of Thailand interannual sea level be weakly coupled to large-scale indices?

### Takeaway
Several well-supported physical reasons plus statistical ones: (1) the GoT responds instantaneously to *local* wind/curl, unlike the SCS basin; (2) ENSO wave signals enter the SCS on its eastern side (Luzon/Mindoro) and are strongest along the eastern boundary, not on the shallow western Sunda Shelf; (3) the GoT western boundary (Ko Lak) appears more IOD- than ENSO-sensitive, so no single index captures it; (4) ENSO's SCS imprint is seasonally phase-locked, which dilutes year-round linear coherence; (5) the climate signal (few cm) is small relative to the residual noise left after removing a ~21 cm annual cycle that itself varies year to year.

### Cited Findings
- GoT basin ADT adjusts with no lag to local wind-stress curl, "a different wind-associated dynamic underlies the GoT compared to the rest of the SCS" (SCS: ~40-day decay timescale) — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- Western-boundary GoT sea level is controlled by wind curl ~280 km to the north (80% variance explained), suggesting coastal-trapped Kelvin waves internal to the gulf — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- Interannual GoT circulation: IOD dominates the western boundary current and the 8°N southern boundary; ENSO correlates with the interior. Zonal wind stress along the western boundary correlates with DMI up to r = −0.75. Westward (eastward) wind-stress anomaly in positive (negative) IOD — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- The mechanism of low-frequency interior ADT variability "cannot be explained by the local wind stress and wind stress curl" and remains unclear — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf)
- ENSO's oceanic pathway into the SCS is via the Mindoro and Luzon straits on the east; Karimata Strait (the shallow Sunda Shelf exit adjacent to the southern GoT) transport is not significantly correlated with Niño3.4 — [Liu, Feng & Wang 2011](https://per.marine.csiro.au/staff/Ming.Feng/Publications/2011LiuJO.pdf)
- ENSO's effect is strongest on the southeastern SCS coastal boundary and weak in the interior because ENSO wind-curl anomalies and local winds can damp SLA variability — [Yang & Fok 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1467164/full)
- Rossby waves radiated from the eastern boundary dominate eastern-SCS sea-level variability; interannual SCS variability "shows a strong seasonality" — [Cheng et al. 2016](https://pol.ouc.edu.cn/2020/1028/c18673a304945/page.htm)
- Non-steric (mass) sea level dominates the coastal SCS while steric dominates deep water (Cheng & Qi 2010, as cited); and Yang & Fok note that their equatorial-Pacific NSSL (non-steric) PC is ~orthogonal to ENSO SST, i.e. "weak or even no ENSO-related signals are present in NSSL" — [Yang & Fok 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1467164/full)
- GoT seasonal sea level is a mass signal (GRACE) — [Wouters & Chambers 2010](https://www.knmi.nl/research/publications/analysis-of-seasonal-ocean-bottom-pressure-variability-in-the-gulf-of-thailand-from-grace)
- Annual-cycle amplitude in the SCS varies interannually by up to 63% (annual) and 45% (semiannual) — [Amiruddin et al. 2015](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/511518/)
- Fort Phrachula tide gauge has a subsidence-driven artificial rise (groundwater extraction) and is ~20 km inland — [Anutaliya 2023](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/19/335/2023/os-19-335-2023.pdf); vertical land motion must be corrected (GPS) to obtain absolute rates (e.g., Ko Lak absolute 3.0 ± 1.5 mm/yr for 1940–2004; altimetry at Ko Lak 3.6 ± 0.7 mm/yr for 1993–2009; Sattahip 5.0 ± 1.3, Ko Sichang 4.5 ± 1.3, Ko Mattaphon 4.4 ± 1.1 mm/yr) — [Trisirisatayawong et al. 2011 summary](https://tccnclimate.com/เอกสารงานวิจัย/เอกสารตีพิมพ์/item/147-sea-level-change-in-the-gulf-of-thailand-from-gps-corrected-tide-gauge-data-and-multi-satellite-altimetry); post-2004 earthquake (Sumatra–Andaman) co/post-seismic subsidence is a known issue for Thai gauges (general knowledge; not verified in this session).
- Luu et al. obtained r ≈ −0.64 to −0.73 using **annual means** — [Luu et al. 2015](https://os.copernicus.org/preprints/11/1519/2014/osd-11-1519-2014.pdf) — whereas monthly residuals retain intraseasonal wind noise.

### Inferences
- Physical decoupling (well supported): The GoT is a shallow (~40 m), wind-dominated, quasi-barotropic system whose sea level tracks local wind stress/curl almost instantaneously. Large-scale indices affect it only to the extent that they change local winds; that link is indirect, regionally heterogeneous, and shared between ENSO, IOD and monsoon variability. Planetary-wave (Rossby/Kelvin) pathways that carry ENSO into the deep SCS do not propagate efficiently onto the broad shallow Sunda Shelf (friction, shallow depth; inference consistent with Liu et al.'s null Karimata result and Yang & Fok's weak-interior/strong-eastern-boundary pattern).
- Index mismatch (moderately supported): Ko Lak is on the western boundary where Anutaliya finds IOD dominance and ENSO weak (R = 0.14); ENSO and IOD often co-occur with compensating or reinforcing wind effects, so coherence with any single index is diluted. A multi-index regression or partial coherence would be more appropriate than single-index wavelet coherence.
- Seasonal phase-locking (moderately supported): ENSO's SCS imprint is strongest in specific seasons (e.g., El Niño-decaying summer, 6-month lag per Cheng et al. 2016). Removing a fixed climatology and computing year-round linear coherence smears this. Analysing seasonally stratified anomalies (e.g., DJF and JJA separately) or modulation of the annual-cycle amplitude might reveal a link that full-series WTC misses.
- Signal-to-noise (inference): if ENSO contributes ~±3 cm (Luu et al. east-coast value) and the residual contains interannual modulation of a ~21 cm annual cycle (up to 63%, i.e. several cm) plus wind-driven intraseasonal noise, the climate signal is at or below the noise level for monthly data; with ~30–50 years of record only ~6–12 ENSO cycles are sampled, so 2–7-yr coherence significance is hard to reach.
- Data artefacts (inference): residual vertical land motion (non-linear post-2004 subsidence), gaps and datum shifts can introduce low-frequency variance unrelated to climate, further lowering coherence. Ko Lak is relatively favourable (good altimetry collocation per Trisirisatayawong et al. 2011) but should be checked.
- Speculative: Monsoon indices might also fail because GoT set-up depends on the regional wind component over the SCS/GoT (direction and curl at specific locations), not on broad monsoon-rainfall or large-domain shear indices.

### Gaps
- No modelling study found that explicitly quantifies damping/blocking of ENSO Rossby/Kelvin signals on the Sunda Shelf and into the GoT.
- No published direct wavelet-coherence analysis of GoT tide gauges vs climate indices was found to compare against our result.

## Q4. Studies using Thai Gulf tide gauges (Ko Lak, Sattahip, Ko Sichang, Songkhla, Fort Phrachula) with ENSO/PDO/monsoon

### Takeaway
Thai tide-gauge studies focus overwhelmingly on long-term trends and vertical land motion, and on seasonal circulation; ENSO is usually mentioned qualitatively. I found no study reporting quantitative tide-gauge-based ENSO/PDO correlations for Ko Lak or other GoT stations.

### Cited Findings
- Trisirisatayawong, Naeije, Simons & Fenoglio-Marc (2011, Global Planet. ## Q5. Andaman Sea vs Gulf of Thailand sea-level variability

### Takeaway
The Andaman coast is connected to the Indian Ocean wave guide (equatorial Kelvin waves → coastal Kelvin waves around the Bay of Bengal), so it carries larger, more coherent IOD/ENSO interannual signals (order ±15–20 cm reported for the Bay of Bengal/Andaman), whereas the GoT is a semi-enclosed shallow Pacific-side shelf sea dominated by a large wind-driven annual cycle. The Andaman tidal/seasonal water level is "more stable" than the GoT.

### Cited Findings
- In the Bay of Bengal and Andaman Sea, ENSO alters MSL within ±15 cm and IOD within ±20 cm (Aparna et al. 2012; Sreenivas et al. 2012) — cited in [Luu et al. 2015](https://os.copernicus.org/preprints/11/1519/2014/osd-11-1519-2014.pdf)
- Malacca Strait has two seasonal peaks (May, Nov; ±15 cm, linked to the South Asian–Indian monsoon) and IOD correlation −0.5 (±2 cm), while the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia (GoT side) has a single annual cycle from the East Asian–Western Pacific monsoon and no significant IOD signal — [Luu et al. 2015](https://os.copernicus.org/preprints/11/1519/2014/osd-11-1519-2014.pdf)
- IOD predominantly drives interannual SLA in the eastern Indian Ocean, ENSO in the SCS; PDO governs decadal SLA especially in the NW SCS (Mohan & Vethamony 2018, Clim. Dyn. 50:3195–3217, doi:10.1007/s00382-017-3800-0) — cited in [Yang & Fok 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1467164/full)
- Andaman water levels more stable than GoT; upper GoT more variable than lower GoT — [PSU Knowledge Bank](https://kb.psu.ac.th/items/125ba56c-af90-4268-87e3-9d6029fac4a4)

### Inferences
- If the wider project finds stronger index coherence at Andaman stations (e.g., Ko Taphao Noi, Phuket) than at Ko Lak, that contrast is physically expected: the Andaman coast is on an open-ocean wave guide fed by the equatorial Indian Ocean, the GoT is not on any comparably efficient open-ocean wave guide.
- Interestingly, Anutaliya (2023) reports IOD-correlated winds on the GoT western boundary, so an IOD signal at Ko Lak would be atmospheric (wind) rather than oceanic-wave-borne (speculative).
